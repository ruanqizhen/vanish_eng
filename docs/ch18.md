# Chapter 18: The Endless Night

## The Night Poured Out

Vera Ashworth smiled.

It was a laugh squeezed from deep in the throat — cold, crystalline. Carrying the weariness and chill of years. She raised the ancient celadon thermos. Slowly, methodically, she brushed the warm fog from its rim. She looked like a dispatcher who'd just finished a day's work. Quietly watching the evening darkness.

Behind her — the entire sky of Grayharbor was tearing apart.

Not torn by external force. It was tearing from the inside — like an old cloth soaked in grease, unraveling at its weakest points. The clouds collapsed inward. They revealed a disturbing deep-violet void beyond. No stars. No moon. Only a heavy darkness slowly churning. Expanding. Like the breathing of a colossal beast stirring from sleep.

Cole tried to move. He found himself frozen in place. The ground beneath his feet had turned soft and viscous — like the skin of something alive, breathing. He looked down. The asphalt was melting. Becoming a deep black, oil-sheened liquid. Within it: countless tiny points of light swimming. Like code. Like some unnameable life-form. The lights moved in patterns — chaotic yet rhythmic — like stars orbiting within a compressed universe.

"Cole." Vera's tone was still that cool, gentle sigh. She tilted the celadon thermos slightly. Mouth downward. "You've delivered so many orders for me. I always wanted to thank you."

The thermos tilted.

What poured out wasn't water.

It was *night.*

Pure black. Absolute. All-light-devouring night. It poured forth like a physical substance. It struck the ground but didn't splash — spreading instead like ink dropped into clear water, racing outward in every direction. Where it passed: streetlamps' light was devoured. Not extinguished — *eaten* from the center outward, curling inward, shrinking, vanishing. Building silhouettes dissolved. Those colossal structures of steel and concrete melted under the night's shadow. Warped. Like melting wax figures. The sky darkened from its edges toward its center at visible speed. The blackness was alive. Greedily swallowing the last of light.

Cole found breathing difficult. Not from lack of oxygen. The air itself was thickening. This blackness wasn't just visual darkness — it was thick with weight. Filling the entire space. Every breath drew something viscous into his lungs. Cold. Damp. Carrying a strange scent of rust and old book-pages.

The citizens of Grayharbor — in a single instant — became puppets. A middle-aged woman outside a convenience store endlessly looped: *"My kid is still at daycare — I need to pick him up —"* A teenage girl repeated into a dead phone: *"Mom, I'm not coming home for dinner tonight —"* Every ten seconds — everything reset. Rewound. Replayed.

Vera's time-recursion prison.

"I wrote the fates of two million, three hundred thousand people." Vera's voice was quiet. Almost conversational. "I made Grayharbor into an offering. A city consecrated to a god."

She looked at Dorian when she said this. Her gaze — not at an enemy. At someone who should have died long ago. And yet still lived.

Cole didn't have time to parse that look.

He was counting.

The time loop. Ten seconds. Reset. Ten seconds. Reset. In the zero-point-one-second gap between resets — he had a window. He seized the delivery box Old Pete had left him. It glowed in the darkness with light independent of the timeline.

His fingers found a receipt pressed at the very bottom. Edges yellowed. Stained with dried blood.

*BBQ pulled pork × 2. Extra sauce. Total: $12.00.*

Below: signatures.

Eleanor Vance. Neat. Precise.

Thomas Vance. Crooked. Like someone who rarely wrote anything, using all his strength to finish.

His parents. Their signatures. On a fifteen-year-old delivery receipt.

The light from the receipt tore through Vera's domain. She let out a sound — shock and fury mixed. Code-tentacles erupted from the earth. Dorian threw himself between them and Cole. His saber flashed. Cutting. Holding.

"GO!" Dorian roared.

Cole tore the receipt open. A spacetime vortex ripped through the darkness. Sucked both of them into a passage of light.

Vera's final image: on that elegant face, at last — a crack. Not rage. Something. Complex. Fleeting.

Then darkness swallowed everything.
