# Chapter 1: The Order That Shouldn't Exist

## The Seventeenth Order in the Storm

The rain hammered against his helmet like someone emptying bucket after bucket from above.

Cole Vance pulled his scooter to the curb and fished out his phone to check the time — 1:40 AM. The screen was covered in water droplets, the touch response glitching. He wiped it with a soaked sleeve just to make out the information. When the screen lit up, he caught a glimpse of his wallpaper — a photo of himself at age five with his parents, the only surviving family portrait. His mother's smile pierced through the damp pixels and the long years, still sharp. He looked at it dozens of times a day. He'd long since gone numb to it. Only occasionally, in the gaps between red lights, would his thumb drift unconsciously across the screen, tracing his mother's face.

A system dispatch.

This was his seventeenth order of the night. Per platform rules, running this one would secure the "Courier Perfect Attendance Bonus" — a hundred and fifty bucks. Combined with the base delivery fees, it would just cover next month's rent. The landlord had already sent the overdue notice last week, each message colder than the last. The most recent one said flat-out: Pay up or I'm changing the locks.

His phone buzzed again. A text from Jamie Holt's icon sat unread.

He swiped it open. Jamie had sent a screenshot — a podcast recording interface, the title bar reading *"Grayharbor Courier Disappearances Investigation"*, the waveform barely a few seconds in. Below it, a single line:

"You seeing this? New project. Grayharbor — seventeen couriers gone missing in three years, all during late-night runs. Not a single one found. You're out there delivering, bro. Watch you don't get dispatched to another dimension lmaooo."

Cole typed two letters without expression: "Fuck. Off."

He shoved the phone back into its waterproof pouch and twisted the throttle.

He didn't have time to shelter from the rain. He tapped **Accept**.

The screen lit up. The order information loaded.

**Order #202405290134**
**Pickup: Mama Chen's BBQ Shack, Old Quarter**
**Item: Pulled Pork BBQ Plate (Mild) × 1**
**Delivery Address: End of Cliffside Road — Number 13**
**Notes: Leave at door. Do not knock.**

Cole stared at the address field for three solid seconds.

Cliffside Road. He knew that road. A lane at the westernmost edge of the Old Quarter, beyond which lay the demolition zone they'd fenced off with blue corrugated steel last year — supposedly for some wetland park the city was building. He'd run deliveries there a few times before, never further than the old apartment blocks in the middle stretch.

But "the end of Cliffside Road" — that phrasing was strange.

Stranger still was "Number 13."

He'd been working as a courier for almost three months. Every street in this zone, every apartment complex, every office tower — even if he hadn't delivered there personally, he'd at least heard the name. But "Number 13"? Never once had it come up in anyone's conversation.

Cole frowned and, out of habit, opened the navigation app on his phone and typed "13 Cliffside Road."

On the map, Cliffside Road's label stopped at the middle section and went no further. Beyond that, a gray patch without any markings — no roads displayed, no buildings. Like a draft that someone had taken an eraser to.

He tried again. Different map apps. Even satellite view. Same result — as if the road simply didn't exist.

"What the hell?"

Cole muttered a curse, exited navigation, and glanced at the order again. The info was still there. The system acceptance countdown had twenty seconds left.

He didn't have much time to hesitate.

Late-night orders like this were scarce. Refusing one meant a point deduction. Deductions hit your acceptance rate. A low acceptance rate meant the system stopped sending you the good orders. A vicious cycle. He'd been a programmer — he understood this algorithm logic intimately. The platform used a precision feedback mechanism to domesticate every courier into a 24/7 labor node.

The data told you when to go online. The data told you which orders to take. The data told you which route to follow.

You didn't need to think. You only needed to execute.

But he wasn't a programmer anymore — not really. Three months ago, he'd been "graduated" from that near-unicorn tech company — HR's term was "optimization restructuring," which was a fancy way of saying laid off. He'd been among the first wave out the door in his entire department. Took the severance package, figured he'd find another gig soon enough. Then he discovered the entire industry was "optimizing."

Three hundred résumés in two days for a single job posting.

His savings had hit bottom — and it wasn't just the layoff. The day he got his severance, he'd done the one stupid thing every novice does: dumped most of it into the stock market. Technical analysis, fundamentals, quantitative models — he wrote a pile of Python scripts to analyze candlestick charts. He was a big-data guy. How hard could this be? Three months later, his account was a skeleton. His closest buddies had given him a nickname that stuck: "Penny." Not the coin — the state of his portfolio. Every time they went out for food, someone would slap his shoulder at the bill: "Penny, don't worry about splitting tonight. Save it for the dip."

That was when the landlord's calls started coming more often. He couldn't make rent, so he took the courier gig — at least there was cash coming in every day. He'd never imagined that a man who'd once written distributed-systems middleware at a top-tier company would one day devote his entire mental bandwidth to figuring out how to get a steaming container of food up six flights of walk-up stairs in under ten minutes.

A memory flickered through his mind — minutes earlier, picking up the order at the BBQ shack. On the back kitchen wall hung a faded old photo: a courier in his mid-thirties standing in front of the shop, grinning with simple warmth. The owner, handing over the food, had said offhand: "That's Old Pete. Used to be our regular. Rode for over a decade, then just stopped last year. Before he left, he gave me that box." She nodded toward the worn insulated delivery box on the back of Cole's scooter. "Said someone would come for it someday. That box was his partner for fifteen years. Wouldn't let me throw it out. Said there was something inside it — the most important thing in his life. I asked him what. He didn't say. Just smiled. Later, I found a delivery receipt at the bottom. Two signatures on it. One neat. One crooked — like someone had used everything they had just to finish writing."

Cole had been in a hurry at the time and hadn't thought much of it. But those words lodged in his subconscious like a splinter. That box — it was sitting quietly on his scooter's footboard right now.

He was just a laid-off courier. Once he'd sat in a server room writing code to change the world. Now he delivered pulled pork in the rain to change his rent situation. So what if he was ordinary? Ordinary people were allowed to look up at the sky too.

"Fine. It's just a delivery."

Cole confirmed the order, stuffed the phone back into its waterproof pouch. Before twisting the throttle, he glanced up — almost involuntarily — at the depot office across the street.

The lights were still on. The blinds were half-drawn, a line of warm amber light spilling through.

Three hours ago, he'd pushed open that door to hand in lastcycle's attendance sheet. She'd been sitting behind the old desk, wearing a faded work jacket, her long hair pinned loosely back, refilling a celadon teacup with hot water. The fragrance of white plum blossoms mingled with the printer-ink smell in the air. He couldn't tell if it was the tea or just... her.

"Running nights again?" She hadn't looked up. Her voice was cool and clear, like snowmelt at the tail end of winter.

"Yeah. One short of full attendance."

She finally raised her eyes. They were striking — not in a loud way, but in a quiet one. Deep and still, like two pieces of polished obsidian resting at the bottom of a dark pool. She looked at him for two seconds, then pushed the celadon cup across the desk.

"Drink it before you go. It's cooled."

When Cole took the cup, his fingertips accidentally brushed hers. Cold. As cold as the tea she'd pushed toward him. But she didn't pull away. She only tilted her head slightly, the corner of her mouth curving into a smile so faint it was barely there.

"Cole."

"Yeah?"

"Be careful out there."

Standing at her desk holding that cup of tea, he'd suddenly felt there was something he wanted to say — but he didn't know what it was. Like a half-written block of code where the logic hadn't closed yet, but you could already feel the function's presence.

He drained the cup, set it back on the desk, turned, and pushed through the door into the rain. Behind him came the soft click of her screwing the lid back on — a precise, mechanical sound. Like a component slotting back into place.

Now, sitting on his scooter, Cole looked one last time at that lit window with its half-drawn blinds. Her silhouette was still there — long hair, celadon cup, her gaze fixed on the screen as his courier status flicked from "Online" to "En Route." He didn't know what she was watching. He also didn't know what that faint curve at the corner of her mouth concealed — concern, or something deeper that he didn't want to think about.

He twisted the throttle. The electric scooter emitted a low groan and shot into the curtain of rain.

The storm-soaked Old Quarter looked like abandoned ruins.

Every other streetlamp was out. The ones still working cast thin, jaundiced light onto the wet asphalt, reflecting back as murky halos. The shops along the road had all closed, their steel shutters rolled down to the ground. Only the occasional 24-hour convenience store sign still glowed — a ghostly white bleeding into the rain-fog, like will-o'-the-wisps drowned at the bottom of a river.

Cole threaded his scooter through alley after alley, tires slicing through puddles and kicking up fan-shaped sprays of water.

The wind was fierce. The rain came in sideways, stinging the exposed half of his face.

He squinted, ducking low, trying to shrink himself behind the scooter's modest windshield. His rain poncho had long since been whipped up by the wind. The uniform underneath was soaked through more than halfway, clinging cold and sticky to his skin.

Goddammit.

He swore, but he didn't slow down. The night was deep. One minute earlier meant one minute sooner back home. He lived in a partitioned sublet — eight square meters, but at least there was a working water heater.

The moment the scooter turned onto Cliffside Road, Cole felt something shift.

He couldn't pinpoint what was wrong. The road was still the same road — old apartment blocks to the right, a couple of mahjong parlors on the ground floors, all dark and shuttered now. To the left, a long retaining wall, its top crawling with dead ivy, rainwater streaming down the vines and glinting faintly under the streetlamps.

But the atmosphere was wrong.

Too quiet. Excessively quiet.

Cutting through the city earlier — even at this dead hour — you could still hear the distant rumble of a car, or the muffled sound of a TV from someone's window. But after entering Cliffside Road, all of those sounds had vanished. As if someone had lowered a giant glass dome over the entire street, sealing it off from the outside world.

Nothing left but the rain and the low electric hum of the scooter's motor.

Cole instinctively eased off the throttle.

He looked ahead. Cliffside Road wasn't long — he knew that from prior runs. Maybe three or four hundred meters of alley. At his speed, he should've been able to see the blue steel barricade at the road's end by now.

But he couldn't.

Ahead, the road stretched straight on, the streetlamps' glow fading into the rain-fog in the distance, blurring but never terminating.

*Something's wrong.*

Cole slammed the brakes. The scooter skidded half a meter on the wet road before stopping.

He checked his phone.

Signal bars: **0**.

No service.

"You've got to be kidding me."

He raised the phone, turned in different directions. Nothing. Cellular data wouldn't connect. The GPS had failed entirely — on the map, his position icon had become a gray dot with no labels around it, as if he'd drifted beyond the map's coverage entirely.

A chill climbed from the base of his spine, up his back, all the way to the back of his skull.

He knew this feeling. It was the instinctive reaction of a programmer — the body responding before the brain — when a system started displaying behavior that didn't match expectations. The last time he'd felt this was in the company's production environment, watching a piece of never-tested code detonate an entire microservice cluster, millions of user requests timing out in thirty seconds flat.

But that was a production outage. That was something he could understand.

This — this was beyond understanding.

He reassessed his surroundings.

The road was still the road. The apartment blocks to the right still stood, their windows black holes without a trace of light. The retaining wall extended to the left, dead ivy clinging, rain continuing to fall.

But everything was different now.

He began to notice the details — the "wrongness" in the details — the things that had flickered past his awareness earlier and been dismissed. Like the apartment windows: every single one was shut, but in some of them, the curtains were stretched taut, as if something inside was gripping them, pulling with unnatural strength. Like the dead ivy on the wall: some of the vines weren't growing upward — they were growing *sideways*, as if something was dragging them toward the wall.

Like — the streetlamps.

He looked up at the nearest lamp.

The light wasn't shining downward.

It was *flat*.

The beam shot out horizontally, as if refracted by something, hitting the wall dead-on and throwing a patch of pale, sickly illumination across its surface. Meanwhile, the street below — the street that *should* have been lit — was drowning in complete darkness.

Cole's heartbeat picked up.

He took several deep breaths and forced himself into calm.

*Don't panic. First, figure out what's happening.*

He'd spent three years as a backend developer. When a system exhibited unexpected behavior at runtime, the first priority wasn't to reboot — it was to gather information. Identify the root cause.

He checked his phone again.

No signal. GPS dead. Time — time showed *what*? He brought the screen close. When he made out the numbers, his fingers went rigid.

The time: **01:41**.

From the moment he'd confirmed the order, time had frozen. The seconds on the display were still ticking, but the minute and hour digits seemed nailed in place. Motionless.

01:41.

He'd accepted the order at 1:40. At least two or three minutes had passed since then. But the display had only advanced by one minute.

Even stranger — the order information was still on screen.

Not "waiting" — the page itself was frozen like a static image. He hit back. Hit refresh. Nothing responded. The order number, the item, the delivery address — every word was nailed steadily in place, radiating an incontrovertible certainty.

A preposterous thought surfaced in his mind:

*This order wasn't dispatched by the platform.*

Or rather —

*This order doesn't belong to this world at all.*

Cole bit down hard on the tip of his tongue. The spike of pain brought a brief clarity.

"Forget it. Just run the damn order."

There was no way back now. No signal meant no calling for help. No turning back. Ahead was the unknown. Behind was the road he'd already traveled — but when he glanced back, the end of Cliffside Road he'd come from was already swallowed by thick fog. Visibility: less than five meters.

When had the fog rolled in?

But he no longer had a retreat.

He twisted the throttle. The scooter inched forward.

## The Mansion in the Mist

The fog thickened.

Cole cranked his headlight to maximum brightness, but the beam was devoured by the fog, penetrating at most three or four meters before dissipating. He rode through the mist at minimum speed, navigating almost entirely by instinct.

But strangely, he no longer needed to judge direction.

Every time a fork or turn appeared ahead, the scooter would — as if guided by some force — naturally lean toward the correct path. He hardly needed to steer. His hands rested lightly on the handlebars, and the scooter guided itself.

The sensation was deeply unsettling.

It was like riding a streetcar on invisible rails — the rails themselves unseen, but the car never deviating from the track.

Cole experimented: he wrenched the handlebars hard to one side.

The scooter let out a piercing shriek. Its tires juddered violently on the wet road surface, as if struggling against some restraint. Then he felt an immense resistance — like an invisible hand was shoving the front wheel back onto its original heading.

His heart clenched.

*Something is controlling my route.*

No — not "something." Some kind of *rule*. A force intervening in physical space, forcefully "calibrating" him onto a predetermined path.

A word surfaced in his mind: **pipeline**.

In programming, a pipeline was a unidirectional data-flow mechanism — data entered at the input, passed through a series of processing stages, and exited at the output. No branches. No choices.

He was now the data inside the pipe.

And the pipe's endpoint — was Number 13.

He tried again. And again.

Brakes — squeezed. The scooter didn't decelerate. Throttle — released. The scooter kept moving forward. The scooter's control logic had been overridden. His inputs were completely silenced. Only one action remained: *forward.* Forward, along that invisible rail. Always forward.

Fear came in like the tide.

Cole's breathing turned rapid and shallow. A tightness spread across his chest. He ripped off his helmet visor, hoping the cold wind would shock him alert, but the fog and rain mixed together as they hit his face, suffocating him further.

He closed his eyes. Opened them.

The fog. Still there. The road. Still there. He was still on thiscareening scooter.

*Steady. Steady.*

He processed the fear the programmer's way — treated it as an exception, threw it to the side, and continued executing the main process.

Emotion couldn't solve problems. Logic could.

He put his helmet back on. Adjusted his seating. Let his body relax. If he couldn't control the direction, then he'd let the system carry him. He needed to conserve his strength for whatever was waiting at the destination.

The scooter threaded through the fog for an indeterminate time.

The surroundings began to change.

The streetlamps vanished. The high walls flanking the road disappeared. In their place: open, overgrown vacant land, weeds bent low by the rain. Occasionally, a few twisted trees loomed in the distance, their branches contorting into strange shapes in the downpour.

The fog gradually thinned.

And then Cole saw it.

It shouldn't have been there.

At the farthest edge of the vacant lot, a gray-white building jutted out of nowhere. Its style clashed with everything around it — a colonial-revival mansion, three stories tall. Six Roman columns supported a triangular pediment at the entrance. The windows were narrow gothic arches, inset with stained glass. The walls were engulfed in dark green ivy, the vines as thick as arms, as if they'd been growing for decades.

The entire building exuded an aura of age, dampness, and cold.

As if it had been cut whole from some forgotten spacetime and pasted onto this patch of wasteland.

Cole's scooter decelerated on its own and stopped before him.

He looked up at the mansion's front door.

The door was dark brown. Wood. Its surface bore a layer of dark red lacquer, cracked like dried blood. A slit of dim yellow light leaked through the transom window above it — someone was inside.

Mounted on the wall beside the door was a brass plaque. It had oxidized to a verdigris green, but the engraved lettering was still legible:

***Number 13***
***Est. 1903***

Cole dismounted. His legs had gone slightly weak.

He looked down at the delivery box on the scooter's footboard — the yellow insulated container seemed jarringly out of place now. Like a label stuck on the wrong package.

BBQ pulled pork. Mild.

An ordinary food order. Over the past two months, he must have delivered the same thing at least two hundred times.

But now, this order carried a different weight. It was an item that hadcross a map that didn't exist, through time that didn't flow,escorted by an invisible force. It wasn't a delivery — it was a *messenger*.

Cole's hands were trembling slightly.

He took several deep breaths, bent down, and opened the delivery box. He pulled out the white plastic bag. The container inside was still warm — he could feel the heat through the plastic.

*The food is real.*

That, at least, meant the restaurant had actually prepared the order.

Holding the bag, he walked toward the mansion door, step by step. Rain struck his face, and he didn't wipe it away.

The flagstone path beneath his feet was badly cracked. Moss filled the gaps, slick and wet. He walked slowly — partly because of the treacherous footing, partly because he wasn't sure he actually wanted to see what was behind that door.

But he had to.

He'd been delivered here. Data in a pipeline. The data had to be handed off at the endpoint.

Cole reached the door. Raised his hand. Hesitated for one second. Then knocked three times.

The sound against the wood was muffled. Like knocking on a thick wall.

No response from inside.

He waited ten seconds. Knocked again, three times. Harder.

Still no response.

Cole glanced back at the vacant land behind him. The fog had closed in again. He could no longer see the road he'd arrived on. His scooter sat at the fog's edge like an abandoned skeleton.

He turned back, ready to knock a third time — this time, he was going to hammer the door with his fist.

The door opened.

No sound. No creak of hinges turning. No click of a latch releasing. That heavy door simply swung inward — silent, frictionless.

The light from inside spilled through the gap. Dim. Amber.

Cole saw the person who had opened the door.

Or rather — he saw the *thing*.

It was human-shaped.

Roughly five-foot-ten. Wearing a dark gray trench coat — thick material, maybe wool — its surface damp in patches from the rain. Black slacks. Leather shoes.

The clothes were normal. They looked like something anyone might wear on a rainy night like this.

But the face —

Cole's pupils contracted violently.

The face had no features.

In their place was a sheet of white printer paper, affixed where a face should have been.

A4 size. Ordinary office paper. Slightly creased at the edges. A small corner damp from the rain. The surface was blank — no writing, no image, no markings whatsoever.

It was a person wearing a paper mask for a face.

Or — the paper *was* its face.

Cole stood rooted to the spot. All the blood in his body felt like it had been drained out.

He'd seen plenty of strange people — drunk customers deep in the night, shut-ins who hadn't opened their doors in three days, the wildly uninhibited guy who answered his delivery wearing nothing but boxers — but he had never seen a person with a piece of printer paper for a face.

His brain, trained as a backend architect, spun through round after round of possible explanations.

Performance art? Cosplay? Club event? Mental illness?

None of them held.

Because that paper — it wasn't in *front* of the face like a mask. He could see the edges, and there was no gap between paper and skin. The sheet was adhered directly to the facial surface, completely covering the positions where eyes, nose, and mouth should have been.

How did a person without eyes see him? How did a person without a mouth speak?

Yet this person — this trench-coated, paper-faced person — stood before him, head tilted slightly, as if sizing him up.

The face had no eyes. But Cole could feel its gaze.

The sensation of being watched emanated from behind that blank white surface, settling on him. An icy coldness penetrated straight through to the bone.

Cole's throat had gone dry.

He raised the delivery bag. His voice came out hoarse: "Your... your delivery."

The Paper-Face didn't move. Didn't nod. Didn't reach out to take it.

They stood like that in the dim amber light, locked in a silent standoff.

Rainwater dripped from the edge of Cole's poncho, pelting the flagstones in a fine spray. The wind stirred the wind chimes hanging from the portico, drawing a few crisp notes.

Then — the paper moved.

Not the body. The paper itself — that sheet of white A4 — began to dimple inward on its surface, as if fingers were pressing from the other side, leaving a shallow depression.

The depression was shaped like a spiral.

Cole's eyes went wide. He tried to step back, but his feet werenailed to the ground.

The depression on the paper deepened. Widened. As if the face beneath it was collapsing, contracting, folding in on itself. The entire surface of the paper became crumpled. The wrinkles converged toward the center. Until finally — the center of the paper collapsed into a **black hole**.

The black hole began to rotate.

Cole could see it clearly — that hole, like a miniature door, opening at the center of the paper. Through it, he glimpsed something:

Architecture. Dark wooden corridors. Oil paintings on the walls. A shattered crystal chandelier overhead. And —

Footsteps.

From the depths of that door, footsteps approached.

Heavy. Slow. Rhythmic. Growing closer.

Someone — or something — was walking out from the interior of the mansion.

And that paper-faced human shape — it was just a doorman. A door that breathed.

Something inside Cole's brain went *click* — the self-preserving memory-overflow protection mechanism he'd developed during his programming years, for when the sensory inputs exceeded capacity. Every sense was running at overload. Every nerve was stretched to the breaking point.

He stared at that black hole. At the darkness in its depths. At the footsteps drawing closer.

He couldn't speak.

## The Old Photo in the BBQ

The footsteps stopped at the edge of the black hole.

Then — a hand extended out.

The hand was very white. Not a healthy pale — a *dead* white. Like a sheet of paper that had never seen sunlight. The fingers were long. Knuckles distinct. Nails neatly trimmed. But under the dim yellow light, they held an unnatural greenish-gray cast.

The hand passed through the black hole in the paper as if passing through a window.

It reached toward Cole — not to grab him, but with its palm open. A *give me* gesture.

The motion was elegant. Composed. Carrying an air of ritual that brooked no refusal.

Cole looked at the delivery bag in his hand. Then at the hand extending from the black hole.

*Fuck.*

Those were the only two words left in his brain.

He'd been a programmer. He'd written more `if-else` branches than he could count. But no block of code he'd ever written could handle *this*. If this were a test case, he'd mark it as "Unreproducible — Environment Anomaly" on the first line and close the ticket.

But this wasn't a test case. This was real.

He had to make a choice.

Cole gritted his teeth and held out the delivery bag.

The hand took it. The fingers closed around the plastic. The motion was slow, as if weighing the heft of an ordinary food delivery.

Then the hand withdrew into the black hole.

The hole in the paper sealed. The wrinkles smoothed out. The paper surface returned to flat and blank.

The Paper-Face reshifted into stillness — and if Cole hadn't seen what had just transpired, he might've believed it was all a hallucination.

But the residual water on the ground said otherwise.

When that hand had reached through the hole, it had brought rainwater with it, droplets falling onto the flagstones of the portico and leaving a few dark stains.

Cole stared at those water stains. His mind spun at full speed.

*Physical contact. Tangible mass. Moisture.*

It wasn't a hallucination. Not a ghost. Not a projection. It was an entity with physical substance — one that had simply passed from some space inside the mansion onto the surface of a sheet of paper, through a mechanism he couldn't comprehend.

The mansion's interior structure was nothing like what the exterior suggested. But its physical properties were continuous.

This was a measurable anomaly.

The realization steadied him — slightly. The unknown inspired fear. But an unknown that could be *measured* had a path toward being understood.

He was about to take a step back and begin strategizing his exit when he heard it.

A sound from inside the mansion.

The sound of paper being torn.

*Rrrrip —*

As if someone had torn a sheet of paper in half. The sound squeezed through the door crack, shockingly clear in the rainy night.

A second rip followed.

*Rrrrip —*

Then the sound of a plastic bag being opened. A food container's lid being pried loose.

*They were unpacking his delivery.*

Cole stood there, unsure whether to leave or stay. Per platform rules, once the delivery was made, you snapped a confirmation photo and the order was complete. But his phone had no signal — he couldn't send the photo. And besides — should he really be asking for a five-star rating from a hand that had reached out of a black hole in a paper face for his BBQ pulled pork?

The thought was so absurd he almost laughed.

But the laugh caught in his throat.

Because another sound came from inside the mansion.

Not the tearing of paper.

A voice. Someone speaking.

A very faint, very thin voice, as if traveling from an impossible distance. Toneless. Utterly devoid of emotion:

*"This taste... is correct."*

A pause.

Then — the light inside the mansion went out.

The dim lamp over the portico extinguished. The light leaking through the door crack vanished. The entire mansion plunged into absolute, total darkness.

Surrounded by the sudden blackness, Cole stumbled backward on instinct.

But his heel caught on something — not flat ground, the edge of a step. He lost his balance. His body tilted backward. He landed hard on his backside on the wet flagstones.

He scrambled up. Backed away several more steps.

Then — the mansion door swung open again. Soundlessly.

This time, it opened wide.

The darkness insidepoured forth like a solid. It swallowed every inch of space on the portico. The darkness was thick — viscous, almost — flowing through the air in slow, languid currents.

Cole stared into that darkness. His heart hammered as if about to burst.

Within the dark, something was moving.

*Shhh — shhh — shhh —*

Like something dragging across the ground.

Then — a hand emerged from the edge of the darkness.

The same dead-white hand. Only this time, it was holding something.

A picture frame.

Black wood. Clearly old — the corners were worn and scuffed. Nestled inside the frame: a photograph. Yellowed with age.

The hand raised the frame. Held it up in front of Cole.

The darkness drew back on either side like theater curtains, revealing the scene within.

The foyer of the mansion: a grand hall with a black-and-white checkerboard tile floor. In the center hung a crystal chandelier — but every bulb was shattered. The wallpaper was dark and floral, moldering and discolored.

And the person holding the frame — the *customer* — stood in the center of the foyer.

Cole finally saw him in full.

A middle-aged man — or at least, he appeared to be. He wore an old-fashioned dark gray suit, his hair slicked back with precision. A pair of metal-rimmed spectacles sat on his face. He looked like a schoolteacher from the 1920s. Or a minor civil servant.

But Cole noticed what was wrong.

The position of the man's eyes — was wrong.

Behind the spectacle lenses were two closed eyelids. No — not closed. *Sewn* shut. Black thread. Coarse stitches. The upper and lower lids had been stitched together like the flap of an envelope being sealed.

And his mouth — the same.

The lips had been sewn shut with identical black thread.

A human shape with sewn-shut eyes and mouth.

The stitches looked old. Some had gone moldy, tinged with green, glinting faintly in the dark.

The man — or rather, the *man-shape* — "looked" in Cole's direction with those sewn-shut eyes. Then he slowly raised the picture frame.

The photo inside the frame was laid bare before Cole.

It hit him like a punch to the gut.

The photo showed a family of three. A young couple, holding a small boy — maybe three or four years old. The husband and wife smiled gently, shyly — the way people smiled in old portrait-studio photos.

The man wore a white shirt. Hair parted to the side. Black-framed glasses.

The woman wore a floral dress. Hair in a ponytail.

The little boy — cradled in his mother's arms — grinned wide, one front tooth missing.

It was him.

It was them.

His parents.

Cole, age five. The family portrait taken at the county photo studio.

He had an identical copy. After his parents disappeared, he'd torn through every photo album in the house and found it — the sole surviving complete family portrait. All these years, he'd carried it on his person. In the inner fold of his wallet.

But —

*How was this photo here?*

*How was it in this mansion that didn't exist?*

*How was it inside an order of BBQ pulled pork?*

Cole's brain went completely blank.

His mouth opened. He tried to speak. But his throat had locked up, as if something wasblocking it. No sound came out.

The man — the one with the sewn-shut eyes and mouth — stood there, silent. Holding the frame aloft.

Cole's gaze drifted from the photograph to the man's face.

And then he saw —

Between the seams of those stitches, inside the man's eye sockets, something was writhing.

Black. Tiny. Like parasitic worms. They burrowed out one by one through the gaps in the sutures, wriggling. A few. A dozen. Dozens. The fine black threadsspread from the stitch holes down the man's cheeks, crawling past his collar, into his suit.

Cole's stomach seized violently.

He bent over and dry-heaved, but nothing came up — all he'd eaten the entire day was a single steamed bun and a cup of soy milk.

The black threads continued to spread.

They looked alive — or like they'd been programmed with instructions, crawling along fixed paths with precision toward the black wooden picture frame.

The threads climbed the edges of the frame. Twined around the surface of the photograph. Then — the people in the photo began to move.

Not the photo itself moving. The people *inside* the photo.

The young woman — Cole's mother — slowly turned her head. Through the yellowed photographic paper, she looked outward, beyond the frame, straight toward him.

Her lips moved.

Soundlessly, she spoke a single sentence.

Cole read the words on her lips:

*"Get out. Now."*

## System Order Accepted

Something snapped inside Cole's brain.

In that instant, all his rationality, all his logic, all his belief in "solving problems with a programmer's mindset" — collapsed.

He no longer thought about why this photo had appeared here. No longer wondered who this sewn-shut man was. No longer cared what those black threads were. No longer questioned why Number 13 didn't exist on any map.

He knew only one thing:

*His mother had told him to run.*

He turned and fled.

His feet slipped on the wet stone. He nearly fell. But he didn't stop. He stumbled toward his scooter, practically threw himself onto it. His hands shook uncontrollably as he twisted the throttle.

The scooter roared. The front wheel lifted, then slammed back down.

He shot into the thick fog.

No direction. No destination. He just pinned the throttle, trying to put as much distance as possible between himself and that mansion. Wind shrieked in his ears. The rain hammered his body — he'd stopped feeling it. His brain was white static, wired to the point of breaking.

Behind him came a sound.

Not footsteps. Not pursuit.

A mechanical, hollow, utterly emotionless voice — like the clatter of an old dot-matrix printer striking paper.

The sound reverberated through the rainy night, cutting through the fog, through the wind, drilling directly into Cole's ears.

He shouldn't have looked back.

But he did.

Number 13's door was now fully open. Like the mouth of a gargantuan beast. The darkness inside churned. The man with the sewn-shut eyes and mouth was now standing in the doorway.

His head was tilted slightly upward.

On that sewn-shut face — across the surface of its skin — text began to appear.

Red text.

As if an invisible dot-matrix printer were printing on his skin, character by character —

The red text looked like it was written in blood. Every stroke was sharp. Clear. The characters began appearing at the forehead and printed downward line by line, layering over the black stitches — like affixing red seals one after another onto a face that had already beensealed shut.

Then — his mouth.

Those sewn-shut lips slowly stretched open.

The black threads snapped.

One by one. Like piano strings breaking. The severed endsbounced in the air, scattering.

The lips pulled apart.

From that mouth emerged — not a human voice.

A synthesized female voice. Like the order-alert tone from a delivery platform, but stretched to ten times its normal speed, pitched low and warped beyond recognition. The sound of a vinyl record spinning at the wrong RPM:

*"Ding — You have a new delivery order — Please process promptly —"*

The sound echoed across the night sky. Again. And again.

*"Ding — You have a new delivery order — Please process promptly —"*

*"Ding — You have a new delivery order — Please process promptly —"*

With every repetition, the sound drew closer.

Cole twisted the throttle wildly. The scooter screamed at the edge of itslimits. The fog grew thicker. Ahead, nothing was visible. He didn't care. He knew only that he had to flee. Get as far away as possible.

Finally, the sound grew distant.

He didn't know how long he'd been riding. Ten minutes, maybe. An hour. Time had lost all meaning inside the fog. His phone had long since died from water damage. Around him: only endless, gray-white mist.

Then — the fog vanished.

As if someone had flipped a switch.

Cole slammed the brakes. Pulled to the roadside.

He looked up. He was on Main Street now. Beside him: a 24-hour convenience store, its sign glowing with warm white light. In the distance, the sound of cars passing. A dog barking somewhere.

Everything was normal.

So impossibly normal.

As if everything that had just happened was nothing but a dream.

Cole sat on his scooter, soaked to the bone, gasping. His phone screen was dead. His clothes were saturated. The delivery box — he stared at the empty insulated container. The smell of BBQ pulled pork that had permeated it — gone. Truly gone.

He'd delivered to a mansion that didn't exist on any map.

He'd handed food to a man whose eyes and mouth had been sewn shut.

That man had found — inside an order of BBQ pulled pork — a photo of Cole's parents, who'd been missing for twenty years.

His mother had told him: *Get out. Now.*

And then — red text had printed itself across that man's face.

**SYSTEM ORDER ACCEPTED.**

**DO NOT REFUSE.**

Cole sat numbly on his scooter. Rainwater dripped from the edge of his helmet.

Slowly, he reached out and pulled his wallet from his pocket.

Inside the inner fold — the family photo he'd carried on his person for twenty years was gone.

In its place: a slip of paper.

A white, A4-sized scrap, folded down to wallet size, tucked into the fold.

His hands trembling, he unfolded it.

On the paper, nothing else. Only a single line —

Red text. Identical to what had printed across that face:

**[ORDER CONFIRMED. AWAIT NEXT DISPATCH.]**

Cole stared at those words for a full minute.

Then he laughed.

It was the laugh of someone past the breaking point. The laugh of someone who had reached the far edge of despair and found nothing left.

He'd been a programmer. He believed in logic. Believed in cause and effect. Believed that everything could be computed and understood.

But now, this slip of paper in his hand was telling him a brutal truth:

*He wasn't the one making deliveries.*

*He was the package being delivered.*

And Number 13 — that mansion — wasn't the endpoint. It was just arelay station.

He lifted his head and looked up at the storm-swept sky. The heavy cloud cover had blotted out every star. The veil of rain above the city hung like a giantshroud, shrouding everything.

He remembered that photo. His mother's silent words.

*"Get out. Now."*

She'd wanted him to run.

But the question was —

*Run where?*

When the entire world's delivery system was lining up orders for him — where could he possibly run to?

Cole folded the slip of paper back up. Tucked it into his wallet. He started his scooter and began the slow ride back toward his rented room.

The rain kept falling.

The road was still long.

And his next order — he had no idea when it would come.

But he knew it would.

**SYSTEM ORDER ACCEPTED.**

**DO NOT REFUSE.**
