# Chapter 9: Breach by Privilege

Under the golden dome, the crystal chandelier refracted brilliant light.

The Meridian Grand Hotel's largest ballroom was dressed to rival any top-tier business summit. At its center, the colossal LED screen scrolled through Cross Group's latest technology-sector strategic roadmap. Below, dozens of round tables were draped in. Silver cutlery gleamed cold under the lights.

But at this moment — not a single pair of eyes was on that screen, whose cost could have fed a small town.

Every gaze was locked on the silhouette that absolutely did not belong at the ballroom entrance.

A delivery courier.

A young man in a fluorescent yellow jacket, helmet still crooked on his head. In his hand: a delivery bag. Standing — in broad defiance of every conceivable social protocol — in the entrance corridor of Cross Group's annual appreciation gala.

Several guests near the door had already risen involuntarily. Their expressions shifted from bewilderment to absurdity.

*— How could a delivery courier break into an event like this?*

The figure of Ada Li materialized from behind a pillar in the hotel lobby.

She wore the dark blue IT uniform of Cross Industries. Her badge swayed faintly under the lights. The data-scarring on her right cheek had been half-covered with foundation — but up close, you could still see the faint blue lines of code pulsing beneath the skin. A permanent mark left by higher-dimensional information erosion.

Twenty seconds earlier, Cole had used the reflective properties of his courier jacket to shake off three lobby security guards and was preparing to infiltrate the ballroom via the side corridor. But Ada Li appeared with precision on his movement vector. Like a silent wall.

"Dorian's memories have been formatted." Her voice was compressed to near-breath — but every word was etched sharp as a knife. "No — worse than formatted. They didn't delete his memories. They *overwrote* them. With a completely new life-script. One in which you don't exist."

Cole's footsteps halted dead.

Ada didn't give him time to digest. She reached into her pocket and pulled out a black access card. Pressed it into his palm. The card still carried the warmth of her hand.

"But taste is the deepest anchor," she said. Her words came fast. "Deeper than memory. Deeper than logic. Deeper than consciousness itself. The hippocampus can be triggered by gustatory signals in under zero-point-three seconds. It bypasses all cognitive lockdown. Feed him something you made with your own hands. In the three seconds he's chewing — his brain will auto-initiate memory recombination."

Cole looked down at the access card. Then up at her. He noticed Ada's right fingertips were trembling faintly — not fear. Some emotion too long. Finally overflowing.

"How do you know all this?"

Ada didn't answer.

She only looked at him — and that gaze held more than Cole could interpret in a single moment. Then she looked away. Turned toward the employee corridor. Her silhouette drew a thin, elongated shadow under the lights.

"Because I tried it. On myself."

Cole braced his hands on his knees. Gasping.

He'd run the entire way — from accepting the order to picking up the food to breaching three layers of hotel lobby security. He hadn't stopped for half a second. Sweat soaked the loose hair across his forehead. Trailed down his brow. He didn't bother wiping it. His gaze crossed the crowd and on the familiar silhouette at the center of the head table.

Dorian Cross.

Tonight he wore a charcoal three-piece bespoke suit. A silver brooch pinned to the peak lapel. His tie clip caught the light in fine. He was turned slightly, red wine glass in hand, speaking to a white-haired elder beside him. His held the calm of absolute command. As if the entire ballroom was his chessboard.

That presence — it was a different man from the one who had bled in the Iron Citadel. Who had struggled on the line between life and death.

But Cole recognized him instantly.

That face. Those eyes. He'd know them if they were burned to ash.

He was meters from the head table. Between them: at least thirty black-suited bodyguards, the party guests, the serving staff, and a chasm wider than any physical distance.

But he was a courier.

And right now — that meant more than any security clearance in the world.

He pulled the BBQ pulled pork from the delivery bag. The container was cold now. Hours old. The sauce had congealed on the meat. But the smell — that cheap, greasy, impossible-to-forget smell — drifted upward.

He pushed through the first row of guests.

A woman in an evening gown stared at him like he'd crawled out of a sewer.

"Security!" someone shouted. "How did he get in?!"

Two bodyguards were already pushing toward him. Cole didn't slow. He tracked their movement arcs through the retinal projection Rust's chip was feeding him — two vectors, converging at forty-five degrees, intercept point roughly six meters ahead.

He adjusted his angle by three degrees. The vectors shifted.

The bodyguards collided with each other.

A champagne tower toppled. Shattered glass. Screams. The whole room was rising now.

Cole was meters from the head table. Someone grabbed his shoulder. He twisted — the courier jacket's slick fabric slipped the grip. He kept walking.

Ten meters. Three more bodyguards formed a wall.

He didn't break stride. He stepped onto a chair. Then a table. Silverware clattered. A woman screamed. He was walking across the table now — directly toward the head table.

The bodyguards couldn't follow. The tables weren't designed for this. They scrambled. They cursed. One drew a weapon. A bullet fired.

It missed.

Not because Cole dodged. Because a waiter, carrying a tray, stepped into the firing line at the exact instant the trigger was pulled. The bullet embedded in the wall behind them. The waiter stood frozen. Unharmed. As if physics itself had redirected the projectile.

*The delivery chain cannot be interrupted,* Cole thought. *Any obstruction is classified as a route obstacle.*

He was meters from the head table now. Three meters. Two.

Dorian Cross rose from his seat.

His security chief — a woman with a expression and an earpiece — was already moving to intercept. Her hand was inside her jacket. Cole could see the faint outline of a holster.

He didn't care.

He was one meter from Dorian now.

And then he did the one thing no security protocol in the world could have anticipated.

He reached into the delivery bag. Grabbed a chunk of cold BBQ pulled pork with his bare hand. And before anyone could react — before the security chief could draw, before the bodyguards could tackle him, before the last gasps could escape the guests —

He shoved it into Dorian Cross's mouth.

## The Taste of Memory

For one second, the world went completely still.

Dorian's body stiffened. His hands — resting at his sides — clenched. The muscle in his jaw jumped. His pupils — dilated.

The security chief had her weapon drawn now. She was screaming something. Cole couldn't hear her. He was watching Dorian's eyes.

In those gray irises — a war was being fought.

The Gestalt Protocol, locking down every memory that contained Cole Vance. The Formatting Protocol, overwriting every neural pathway that led to the Iron Citadel. And against it — a single, pathetic, impossibly stubborn taste. BBQ sauce. Cheap spices. Grease. The flavor of a cold, hours-old delivery.

The flavor that Dorian Cross had first tasted in a prison cell. When a man bound in chains had told him — *"I'm not your enemy."*

Dorian's hand rose. Slowly. His fingers touched his own lips. His eyes — those cold, commanding, CEO eyes — were no longer looking at an intruder.

*"Cole."*

The word was a rasp. Barely audible.

*"The man in the Iron Citadel... it was you."*

Cole's throat locked. He nodded.

"Welcome back," he managed. "Dorian."

The security chief was still shouting. Bodyguards were closing in. The guests were in chaos. But in that one instant — none of it existed. There was only the space between them.

Dorian's gaze was flickering — as if he was trying to hold onto something that kept slipping away. His fingers reached out. Touched Cole's sleeve. The fabric of the courier jacket.

"You're real," he murmured. "I thought... I thought I dreamed you."

"Not a dream."

"I remember... chains. A red thread. You... you said you'd find me."

"I said I'd find you. In every world."

The moment broke.

Dorian's face contorted. His hand flew to his temple. He staggered. The protocols were fighting back — Cole could see it. The Gestalt Protocol was asserting its lockdown. Dorian's memories were being forcibly suppressed again.

But he was fighting it. His eyes — those gray eyes — were blazing with something Cole had never seen in the CEO version of him before. Defiance.

"Don't let them take it again," Dorian ground out. "Don't let them take you —"

And then every light in the ballroom went out.

## The Specter Screen

Darkness. Absolute.

Then — a low hum. Building. The LED screen at the center of the ballroom flickered. Once. Twice. Then blazed to life — but not with the Cross Group strategic roadmap. The screen was blood-red. A single, colossal eye opened on its surface. No pupil. No iris. Just a void ringed in red light.

And a voice — mechanical. Hollow. Utterly inhuman — echoed through the darkness:

*"WEL-COME — TO — MY — BAN-QUET. LET — US — BE-GIN — THE — ROLL — CALL."*

The temperature in the ballroom dropped twenty degrees. The guests' panicked screams died in their throats. A strange stillness settled over the room — not calm, but the paralysis of prey.

The specter on the screen had no face. No features. Only the suggestion of a shape — something vast and hungry, pressing against the boundary between dimensions.

*"FIRST — LET — US — DE-TER-MINE — THE — SORT-ING — OR-DER."*

Lines of code began scrolling down the blood-red screen. Names. Ages. Seat numbers. The Quicksort algorithm — Cole recognized it instantly. It was sorting the guests. Selecting pivots. Partitioning sub-arrays.

The specter was going to kill them. One by one. In algorithmic order.

"Everyone stay calm." A voice cut through the darkness. Calm. Steady. Ada Li stepped forward. In her hand: her phone. The screen was already lit. She'd connected to the ballroom's internal network.

"Dorian still has partial access to the Cross Industries security mainframe," she said quietly to Cole. "His biometrics are still in the system. If we can get him to a terminal —"

"He can barely stand," Cole said.

"I know." Ada's gaze was steady. "That's why you're going to buy me time."

The specter spoke again.

*"FIRST — GER-ALD — MAS-ON. AGE: 52. SEAT: SEC-TION A, ROW 3, POS-ITION 7."*

A man in the audience — a real-estate developer, Cole remembered from the guest list — screamed. His body began to dissolve. From the inside out. Flesh. Bone. Clothing. In three seconds, all that remained was a dark red puddle on the tablecloth.

Cole's programmer brain was already running.

*Quicksort. Age as key. Pivot selection.* He watched the algorithm play out on the screen. The names. The partitions. The alternating farthest-to-nearest order.

"Ada — the screen. It's not a display. It's a data-stream entity. It has a UDP debug port."

Ada was already typing. "I see it. Unencrypted. Exposed directly in the communication architecture."

"If I can inject a logic paradox —"

"Do it," Ada said. "I'll keep the port open."

The specter named its second victim. Third. Fourth. The guests were dissolving one by one. The algorithm was merciless. But Cole was no longer watching the deaths. He was coding.

He pulled up the remnant game panel on his retinal display. His fingers flew across the virtual keyboard. He was writing a pseudocode injection — a logic bomb. The simplest, most brutal paradox in the programmer's arsenal.

*`IF A = B THEN FORMAT TARGET ELSE (A = B AND B = A + 1)`*

A condition that could never be true. An infinite loop.

He injected it into the specter's judgment pipeline through the UDP debug port.

For a moment — nothing.

Then the specter froze.

Its single eye — that ring of red light — flickered. Stuttered. The scrolling code on the screen began to. Faster. Faster. The specter was trapped in the infinite recursion — *Is A equal to B? No. Set A to B. Increment B. Re-judge.* — cycling through the loop endlessly, devouring its own processing cycles.

*"ER-ROR — ER-ROR — UN-REC-OV-ER-ABLE — NAR-RA-TIVE — PAR-A-DOX —"*

The screen shattered.

Not physically — digitally. The blood-red surface cracked into a million fragments of light. The specter let out a final, silent scream and imploded. Data fragments swirled through the ballroom like glowing snow.

And at the center of the swirl — suspended in the air — something gleamed.

A golden, crystalline object. Ringed in rotating Aetheric glyphs. Radiating a light that felt ancient and primordial.

*The Alchemical Kernel.*

Cole stared at it. He didn't know what it was. Not yet. But he knew — with the bone-deep certainty of a programmer looking at a critical system file — that this was important. This was a key.

He reached for it.

And the world went white.
