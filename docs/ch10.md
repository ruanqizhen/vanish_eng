# Chapter 10: Death Sort

The ballroom lights went out.

Not an ordinary power outage — the darkness pressed inward from all sides like a living thing. The dome's crystal chandelier — worth more than most people's homes — flickered its last few stuttering pulses, emitted a piercing electronic whine, and sank into absolute black. The crowd's screams hadn't fully erupted before a deeper terror seized every throat.

Cole gripped the wine glass in his hand. The cold surface was his only reality-anchor.

Three seconds ago, he'd been listening to a real-estate developer boast about a land acquisition on the west side. Three seconds later, the entire world had been plunged into ink.

"Dorian?" He kept his voice low.

"Here." Dorian's voice came from the right. Less than a meter away — but it sounded like it was filtered through a thick wall. "Don't move. I'm right beside you."

Panicked cries and the crash of overturned chairs erupted through the darkness. People were weeping. Shouting for security. Dialing phones — but every sound was swallowed by the darkness, growing distant and distorted.

The screen lit up.

A colossal LED panel — spanning an entire wall. At its center, an unsettling crimson surfaced. Like some liquid substance flowing slowly across the surface. The red deepened. Darkened. Until it coalesced into a shape.

A human silhouette.

More precisely — the shape of a person. But without any detail. No features. No hair. No nails. Like a crude doll sculpted from blood. Its head slowly rotated. It seemed to be *looking* at the hundreds of guests below.

A chill shot up Cole's spine.

That wasn't a human movement. The "head" rotated far beyond the limits of any cervical spine — nearly a full one-eighty. It stopped. Facing a certain direction in the audience.

The specter on the screen spoke.

The voice emerged from the speakers throughout the ballroom. Low. Rasping. Like someone scraping sandpaper across vocal cords. Every word was stretched long. Vibrato that raised goosebumps. The instant that voice scraped across Cole's eardrums, he felt a wave of déjà vu vertigo — not fear, but something deeper. A discomfort that touched the brainstem. He remembered the writhing symbols in Number 13's basement. The violet vapor that had nearly driven him insane. That sensation — the Low-Speech contamination — still had residue here. The Laws had switched, but the scars they'd left on his nerve-endings wouldn't vanish.

*"WEL-COME — TO — MY — BAN-QUET."*

The voice echoed through the vast hall. Eardrums numbed.

*"LET — US — BE-GIN — THE — ROLL — CALL."*

Roll call?

Cole's heart seized. He glanced instinctively toward Dorian. In the darkness, only a blurred silhouette was visible. Dorian's hand had somehow found his wrist. The grip was hard enough to crush bone.

"Everyone, don't be nervous —" The specter's voice suddenly smoothed out, like an old radio abruptly locking onto a frequency. Terrifyingly clear. "I just want to get to know everyone here. When your name is called, please stand up."

The red figure on the screen raised a hand — if you could call it a hand — and pointed toward a section of the audience. Its "fingers" were elongated like melted candle wax. The tips seemed about to pierce through the screen.

*"FIRST —"*

The entire ballroom fell into deathly silence. No one dared make a sound. No one dared move. Hundreds of people in place. Even their breathing was careful.

*"GER-ALD — MAS-ON."*

The name materialized on the screen in blood-red characters. As if written in fresh blood. Each stroke still dripping.

A scream erupted from the crowd. Cole saw a middle-aged man bolt from his seat. Face paper-white. He recognized him — the real-estate developer who'd been boasting moments ago. Gerald Mason.

"No, no, no — not me — why me —"

He stumbled backward, waving his hands frantically. His body knocked over the chair behind him. His bodyguard tried to rush forward — took one step — was hurled backward by an invisible force. Slammed hard to the ground.

Then the dissolution began.

Gerald Mason's body began melting from the inside. As if invisible hands were kneading beneath his skin. His facial contours deformed. Features. Vanished. The skin melted like wax. Muscle and bone lay exposed beneath. He tried to scream — but his throat had already collapsed into a pulp of flesh.

The entire process lasted three seconds.

Three seconds later — nothing remained but a pool of dark red liquid. Slowly spreading across the carpet. The black designer suit. The bespoke shoes. The Rolex on his wrist — all gone. Not even residue.

Only then did the screaming truly erupt.

The ballroom collapsed into chaos. Some charged madly for the exits. Some collapsed to the floor, howling. Some tried to climb through windows. Some knelt and beat their heads against the floor. But everyone who touched a door handle — the instant they made contact — their entire body was engulfed in pale blue flames.

*"AAAAAAHHHHHH —!"*

The first to reach the door was a young woman — not yet thirty. Her evening gown turned to ash in an instant. Her skin curled like paper. Charred. Sloughed off. She rolled on the floor. Struggled. Tried to smother the flames. But the fire seemed to grow from her body. The harder she fought, the brighter it burned. Seconds later, she too was a pool of liquid, seeping into the floorboards' seams.

Cole forced himself to look away.

*Don't watch the deaths. Watch the algorithm.*

He pulled up his mental model. Gerald Mason. 52. Section A, Row 3, Seat 7. The first victim.

The specter was naming them in a specific order. Not random. Not by wealth. Not by prominence. There was a *pattern.*

The second victim's name appeared on the screen: *Ada Li. Age: 25. Section C, Row 9, Seat 2.*

Cole's heart stopped.

He scanned the crowd. Found her — Ada, standing near a pillar. Her face was pale. But her expression was unnervingly calm. As the red light enveloped her, her right arm began to dissolve — the flesh pixelating, revealing the code-threaded bones beneath. Then —

The dissolution stopped.

Midway.

The specter's algorithm adjudicated her as "processed." But Ada's old data-scars — the same scars she'd carried since the Origin Night — interfered with the dissolution. The dissolution halted. Her right arm was permanently scarred with flowing code. But she was alive.

And she was already working. Her left hand pulled out her phone. Tethered into the ballroom's local network. Opening a backdoor for Rust.

"Cole." Ada's voice was rough. But steady. "I'm feeding you data. Find the algorithm's exploit."

Cole's programmer brain shifted into high gear.

*Quicksort.*

The specter was using the Quicksort algorithm. He could see it now — the victims' names and ages laid out like a dataset. The specter was selecting pivots. Partitioning. Naming the median elements from each sub-array.

Gerald Mason, 52 — the global pivot.
Ada Li, 25 — left sub-array's furthest from pivot.
Thomas King, 56 — right sub-array's furthest.
Then alternating — furthest-to-nearest — from each sub-array.

It was mathematically elegant. And mathematically exploitable.

"If I can find the pivot selection logic —" Cole muttered. "If the specter is using median-of-three for pivot selection —"

Then there would be a pattern. A *predictable* pattern. And anything predictable could be broken.

He watched the fourth victim dissolve — a government official. The fifth — a lawyer. The sixth — a wealthy wife.

And then the seventh.

The screen flickered. The name that emerged was not what the algorithm predicted.

*Elaine Yip. Age: 41. Section C, Row 1, Seat 8. Teacher.*

Cole's eyes narrowed. Elaine Yip should not have been next. According to the Quicksort model, the seventh victim should have been —

Wait.

His gaze swept upward. Toward the ballroom's dome. There — concealed in the ornamental relief work — was a stone tablet. Engraved with names. *Seven* names. Including the six already dead. And the seventh: Elaine Yip.

She had seen the tablet.

And the moment she saw it — the algorithm *changed.*

The sorting key shifted. From age to a weighted hash of name and seat coordinates. The rules were rewriting themselves in real time. Whoever looked at the tablet — became part of the algorithm's updated logic.

Cole looked at Ada. "The tablet. It's a rule-modification trigger. Every person who sees it updates the sorting key."

Ada was already typing. "Then we need someone to see it who will break the algorithm. Someone whose parameters will crash the hash function."

Cole understood.

He stood up.

And he looked directly at the stone tablet.
